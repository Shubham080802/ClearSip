# Beverage ingredient roles, body effects, and drinking contexts

Researched 2026-09-28 against primary public-health, regulatory, and professional sources. General education, not individualized dietary or medical clearance. No bulk scraping or undisclosed-formulation reconstruction was performed.

## Recommended presentation

Replace good/bad ingredient verdicts with four explicit fields: **role in the drink**, **what it can do in the body**, **amount and evidence limits**, and **source**. Preserve the exact label wording alongside the explanation. Ingredient presence is not a measured dose; ingredient order is not enough to calculate an exposure. FDA explains that label ingredients generally appear in descending weight order, with some collective declarations and exemptions. [FDA ingredient types](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-and-gras-ingredients-information-consumers/types-food-ingredients).

Use general effects only for identified ingredients. Link any quantitative nutrition statement to the selected US package and serving basis. If a manufacturer publishes an exact flavor composition, store it as separately sourced disclosure, not as text originally printed on the label. Never infer undisclosed components from another flavor, patent, generic recipe, or blog.

## Proposed ingredient copy

### Sugar, sucrose, dextrose, fructose, and high-fructose corn syrup

**Role:** Caloric sweetener. **Body:** Supplies carbohydrate energy; digestible carbohydrates can raise blood glucose. **Context:** Frequent consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages is associated with weight gain, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and dental decay. These population-level associations do not diagnose a person or mean a single drink causes disease. [NIDDK carbohydrate guidance](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/healthy-living-with-diabetes), [CDC sugary-drink evidence](https://www.cdc.gov/nutrition/php/data-research/sugar-sweetened-beverages.html).

Suggested snippet: “Adds sweetness and carbohydrate energy. The amount you drink contributes to your sugar intake and can affect blood glucose. Frequent sugary-drink consumption is associated with several long-term health risks.”

Do not portray HFCS as uniquely toxic compared with equivalent amounts of sucrose: FDA reports no evidence of a safety difference for HFCS 42/55 versus comparable glucose/fructose nutritive sweeteners. [FDA HFCS Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/high-fructose-corn-syrup-questions-and-answers).

### Fruit juice and juice concentrate

**Role:** Juice base, flavor, and potentially a sugar source. “No added sugar” is not “no sugar”: total sugars include naturally occurring sugars. Concentrate is not automatically an added-sugar violation; the FDA definition depends on how much is used relative to equivalent single-strength juice. Use the nutrition panel's total/added sugars, not ingredient-name guesswork. [FDA added sugars](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/added-sugars-nutrition-facts-label), [FDA industry clarification](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/industry-resources-changes-nutrition-facts-label).

Snippet: “Contributes fruit flavor and naturally occurring sugars. Check total sugars even when the label reports no added sugar.” Do not copy sugary-soda disease claims wholesale onto every 100% juice. CDC treats limited amounts of 100% juice as nutrient-containing drinks within calorie limits. [CDC drinking options](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/water-healthy-drinks/index.html).

### Caffeine, coffee, tea, and guarana

**Role/body:** Stimulant; can increase alertness. Excess may cause sleep disruption, anxiety, jitters, palpitations, and digestive symptoms. Guarana adds caffeine; coffee/tea need not list caffeine separately when it is inherent. Natural and added caffeine are handled similarly. For most adults, FDA cites 400 mg/day as not generally associated with negative effects; this is not a target, universal safe limit, or clearance for pregnancy, children, sensitive people, or certain medicines. [FDA caffeine guidance](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much), [NCCIH energy drinks](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/energy-drinks).

Snippet: “Can increase alertness, but may disturb sleep or cause jitters and a racing heartbeat. Add up caffeine from all drinks and other sources; sensitivity varies.” Unknown amount stays unknown; decaf does not mean caffeine-free. FDA advises consultation for pregnancy/breastfeeding and reports medical advice against energy drinks for children/teens.

### Aspartame, sucralose, acesulfame potassium, and steviol glycosides

**Role:** Intense sweeteners; sweetness from small amounts, with few/no calories at typical use. FDA says these generally do not raise blood sugar and supports authorized uses under their conditions. This is not proof of zero insulin response in every person, disease prevention, or unlimited intake. Aspartame contains phenylalanine; people with PKU should avoid/restrict it. High-purity steviol glycosides differ from whole-leaf/crude stevia. [FDA sweetener review](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food).

Snippets: “Sweetens without the usual amount of sugar; FDA supports its authorized use conditions. Amount per drink is not disclosed, so a personal exposure cannot be calculated.” Aspartame adds: “Contains phenylalanine: important for people with PKU.” Do not tag Ace-K as an electrolyte benefit merely because its name contains potassium.

FDA ADIs: aspartame 50, sucralose 5, Ace-K 15 mg/kg body weight/day; steviol glycosides 4 mg/kg/day as steviol equivalents (not 4 mg of every stevia preparation). These are lifetime exposure references, not recommendations to consume that much; do not calculate “safe cans/day” without disclosed amounts. [FDA sweetener review](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food).

WHO's conditional recommendation against relying on non-sugar sweeteners for long-term weight control is not a toxicological ban or a finding that each drink causes diabetes. Observational disease associations can be confounded; its recommendation excludes people with pre-existing diabetes. [WHO recommendation and uncertainty](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-05-2023-who-advises-not-to-use-non-sugar-sweeteners-for-weight-control-in-newly-released-guideline), [WHO scope](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073616).

### Citric acid, phosphoric acid, malic acid, and acidic beverages

**Role:** Acidification/tartness; acidity control may also support preservation. **Body:** Frequent contact with acidic drinks can erode tooth enamel even without sugar. Product pH, buffering, contact frequency, and saliva affect actual erosion; an acid name alone cannot establish the drink's pH or rank its harm. [FDA roles](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-and-gras-ingredients-information-consumers/types-food-ingredients), [ADA dental erosion](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/dental-erosion).

Snippet: “Adds tartness/acidity. Frequently sipping acidic drinks can wear tooth enamel, including sugar-free drinks. This label does not measure the drink's acidity.” ADA suggests reducing frequent exposure and rinsing with water, rather than immediately brushing after acidic drinks. Do not say citric acid is vitamin C: ascorbic acid is a different compound.

### Sodium and potassium salts/electrolytes

Sodium supports fluid balance and nerve/muscle function; high total dietary sodium can raise blood pressure. Label sodium includes contributions from multiple ingredients, not just salt. FDA's sodium DV is 2,300 mg; 5% DV or less per serving is low and 20% or more high. These label references are not individualized targets. [FDA sodium education](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-education-resources-materials/sodium-your-diet).

Potassium supports nerve transmission, muscles, and heart/kidney function. Kidney disease and some medicines can impair handling and cause excessive blood potassium. [NIH ODS potassium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Potassium-Consumer/).

Snippet: “Supplies sodium/potassium ions involved in fluid balance and nerve/muscle activity. Use the nutrition panel to assess the amount; an electrolyte ingredient alone does not prove this drink is needed for hydration.” Sodium citrate can regulate acidity as well; do not infer a therapeutic effect from its name.

### Taurine

Taurine has physiological roles, including bile-acid-related fat digestion. That normal role is not evidence that consuming this drink improves digestion, longevity, heart health, or athletic performance. The NIH infection finding was basic research involving mice, not proof that taurine beverages prevent infections. [NIH taurine research and limits](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-scientists-identify-nutrient-helps-prevent-bacterial-infection).

Snippet: “A compound involved in normal body processes, including bile-acid-related fat digestion. Its presence alone does not establish a benefit from this energy drink; amount and formulation matter.” Separate caffeine's stimulant effects from taurine. NCCIH's older energy-drink review describes mixtures, not isolated taurine efficacy. [NCCIH energy drinks](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/energy-drinks).

### B vitamins

Niacin/B3 helps energy metabolism and cell function; B12 supports blood/nervous-system functions but does not boost performance when intake is already adequate. High supplemental B6 taken long term can damage nerves. High-dose nicotinic acid can cause flushing and other effects; **niacinamide/nicotinamide has a different adverse-effect profile**, so do not apply nicotinic-acid flushing copy to it. [NIH ODS niacin](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Niacin-Consumer/), [NIH ODS B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/), [NIH ODS B6](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB6-Consumer/).

Snippet: “Supports normal nutrient metabolism. Added vitamins do not automatically provide extra energy or make the beverage beneficial; consider the declared amount and your total intake.” Do not turn high-dose supplement harms into an unsupported warning that a normal beverage dose causes liver/nerve disease.

### Vitamin C/ascorbic acid

Vitamin C supports collagen formation, antioxidant activity, iron absorption, and normal immune function. Ascorbic acid can also protect product freshness. A drink containing it is not shown to prevent infection or cure disease. Large supplemental intakes can cause diarrhea, nausea, and cramps; amount matters. [NIH ODS vitamin C](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC/Consumer/).

Snippet: “Vitamin C is involved in collagen formation and normal immune function. This ingredient name does not tell us the vitamin dose or establish a disease-prevention benefit.” Keep citric acid distinct.

### Natural/artificial flavors

The US regulation defines these by source and flavoring function and permits collective declarations. They are **categories, not identifiable single chemicals**. Web research cannot reconstruct a proprietary blend. Natural origin is not a safety guarantee; FDA applies the same safety standard to naturally/artificially derived ingredients. [21 CFR 101.22](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-B/section-101.22), [FDA ingredient explanation](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-and-gras-ingredients-information-consumers/types-food-ingredients).

Snippet: “Adds aroma and taste rather than a defined nutritional effect. This grouped label term does not identify the individual flavoring substances or their doses; their specific bodily effects cannot be inferred. Exact manufacturer disclosures can add detail.” This is a precise explanation of the limitation, not “no information.”

### Benzoate/sorbate preservatives

**Role:** Helps suppress spoilage organisms. Presence alone cannot establish toxicity, disease risk, or nutritional benefit. Sodium/potassium benzoate is not the same ingredient as benzene. Under some conditions benzoate plus ascorbic acid can form small amounts of benzene; product-specific analytical testing is needed to know whether it occurred. Do not infer benzene from citric acid or list it as a hidden ingredient. [FDA preservative roles](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-and-gras-ingredients-information-consumers/types-food-ingredients), [FDA soft-drink chemistry](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/carbonated-soft-drinks-what-you-should-know), [FDA analytical method](https://www.fda.gov/food/environmental-contaminants-food/determination-benzene-soft-drinks-and-other-beverages).

Snippet: “Helps limit microbial spoilage. Its presence is not a measurement of your exposure or evidence that this drink causes disease; the package does not disclose its concentration.” Potassium sorbate is a preservative, not a meaningful potassium benefit unless nutrition data establishes an amount.

### Colors, including caramel color and named dyes

**Role:** Appearance, not evidence of nutritional benefit. FDA authorization is specific to substance/use conditions and can change; natural and synthetic colors both require safety evaluation. Do not label every artificial dye harmful or every natural color harmless, and do not guarantee every historical ingredient is currently authorized. [FDA colors and post-market reassessment](https://www.fda.gov/food/color-additives-information-consumers/color-additives-foods).

Snippet: “Changes the drink's appearance. The color name alone does not establish an individual bodily effect or harmful dose.” Ingredient-specific sensitivity copy needs its own source and known identity, not a generic all-colors allergy/disease claim. Do not infer caramel-color subtype or contaminant level from the label.

### Milk, soy, oat, and protein ingredients

Protein supports tissue structure and body functions. Dairy and plant-based alternatives differ greatly in protein and fortification; measure the selected drink's declared amounts rather than transferring milk's nutrient profile to oat drinks. [FDA milk/alternative comparison](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/milk-and-plant-based-milk-alternatives-know-nutrient-difference).

Milk and soy are major US allergens; severe allergic reactions are possible. Oat is not itself one of the nine major US allergens, but “oat” alone cannot prove a product gluten-free or free of milk/soy cross-contact. Absence of a warning is not proof of absence of risk. [FDA allergen labeling](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/food-allergies-what-you-need-know).

Snippet: “May contribute protein and other nutrients; see the panel for actual amounts. Milk/soy ingredients require attention if you have those allergies.” Lactose malabsorption can cause gas, diarrhea, bloating, and pain; it is distinct from milk allergy, including in lactose-free milk. [NIDDK lactose intolerance](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/symptoms-causes).

For **lactase**, use: “An enzyme that breaks down lactose. It can reduce lactose-related digestive symptoms, but does not remove milk proteins or make a milk drink suitable for milk allergy.” Lactase-product evidence supports the mechanism, not a guarantee about residual lactose in every finished drink. [NIDDK lactase treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/treatment).

### Inulin/chicory fiber and other fibers

FDA recognizes inulin/inulin-type fructans for dietary-fiber declaration under enforcement discretion. That does not assign every fiber all possible benefits or prove a finished drink treats disease. Some people get more gas with high fiber intake; too much at once can trigger gas/bloating, especially with IBS. [FDA fiber Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/questions-and-answers-dietary-fiber), [NIDDK gas](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gas-digestive-tract/eating-diet-nutrition), [NIDDK IBS/fiber](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/irritable-bowel-syndrome/eating-diet-nutrition).

Snippet: “Contributes non-digestible carbohydrate/fiber. The type and amount matter; larger intakes may cause gas or bloating. A fiber ingredient does not establish a disease-treatment benefit from this drink.”

### Carbonated water/carbon dioxide

Snippet: “Creates the bubbles. Fizzy drinks can contribute to gas symptoms in some people; plain carbonation is not a sugar source.” Do not transfer every soda's acidity or health effects to plain sparkling water. [NIDDK gas guidance](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gas-digestive-tract/eating-diet-nutrition).

### Allulose and erythritol

Allulose is chemically a sugar but metabolized differently; it is not a sugar alcohol. FDA permits 0.4 kcal/g and excluding it from total/added sugars while retaining it in total carbohydrate. Thus zero reported sugar does not necessarily mean absence of chemically defined sugars or zero energy. [FDA differently metabolized sugars](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/sugars-are-metabolized-differently-traditional-sugars).

Snippet: “A sweetening sugar metabolized differently from traditional sugar; it contributes less energy. The label may exclude it from total/added sugars, but includes it in carbohydrate.” Avoid a categorical promise about insulin for the complete drink.

Erythritol is a sugar alcohol, not drinking alcohol. Sugar-alcohol-containing products can trigger gas symptoms in some people. [NIDDK gas guidance](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gas-digestive-tract/eating-diet-nutrition). FDA states the 2023 observational evidence did not establish cardiovascular causation and continues monitoring; neither “causes heart disease” nor “proven zero cardiovascular risk” is warranted. [FDA sweetener evidence](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food).

## Additional common label ingredients

These profiles extend the same evidence boundaries: describe the declared substance and its normal role; use the selected product's panel for quantities. A permitted manufacturing use or a normal nutrient function does not establish a personal benefit or an unlimited intake allowance.

### Calcium carbonate/phosphates, phosphorus, and magnesium salts

Calcium supports bones/teeth and normal muscle and nerve activity. Calcium carbonate and calcium phosphate are calcium-containing forms; neither name specifies the elemental calcium delivered by this drink. Phosphorus participates in cell structures and energy processes; phosphate ingredients may also serve formulation functions. Severe chronic kidney disease can impair phosphorus handling. [NIH ODS calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-Consumer/), [NIH ODS phosphorus](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Phosphorus-Consumer/).

Magnesium is involved in normal muscle/nerve function and protein/bone production. The ingredient's compound weight is not its elemental magnesium amount. Impaired kidney function increases the risk from excessive magnesium exposure. High-dose supplement/medicine digestive or toxicity findings should not be portrayed as effects of ordinary beverage amounts; the supplemental magnesium UL is not an all-food magnesium limit. [NIH ODS magnesium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/).

Snippets: “Calcium-containing mineral ingredient. Calcium supports bones and normal muscle/nerve function; see the nutrition panel for the actual calcium amount.” “Magnesium-containing ingredient involved in normal muscle and nerve function. Amount matters; kidney disease can change mineral handling.” “Phosphate ingredient: phosphorus is involved in bones, cells, and energy processes, but the label name alone does not establish its dose or this drink's nutritional benefit.” Do not treat calcium disodium EDTA as a calcium supplement or Ace-K as a meaningful potassium source.

### Riboflavin/B2 and vitamins A, D, E

- **Riboflavin/B2:** Supports cell function and converting food into usable energy. This is not a stimulant or evidence of an extra energy boost from a drink. Snippet: “Vitamin B2 helps the body use nutrients for normal cell function; added B2 does not automatically provide an energy boost.” [NIH ODS B2](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Riboflavin-Consumer/).
- **Vitamin A:** Supports normal vision, immune function, growth, and development. Preformed vitamin A (such as retinyl palmitate) and provitamin carotenoids (such as beta-carotene) are not interchangeable for dosing or safety conclusions. Snippet: “Vitamin A supports normal vision and immune function; the form and declared amount matter.” Do not map preformed-vitamin-A high-dose cautions onto every carotenoid color ingredient. [NIH ODS vitamin A](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/vitamina-consumer/).
- **Vitamin D:** Supports calcium absorption, bones, and normal muscle/nerve functions. Snippet: “Helps the body absorb calcium and supports normal bone function. Its presence alone does not show a disease-prevention benefit.” D2/ergocalciferol and D3/cholecalciferol may appear as exact names. [NIH ODS vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-Consumer/).
- **Vitamin E:** Has an antioxidant role in cells and supports immune function. Tocopherols can also be added to protect food quality; ingredient presence does not measure vitamin nutrition. Snippet: “Vitamin E has an antioxidant role in the body; tocopherols may also help protect product freshness. Check the panel for a declared nutrient amount.” High-dose supplemental bleeding cautions are not a prediction about a normal beverage dose. [NIH ODS vitamin E](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-Consumer/), [FDA antioxidant ingredient role](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-and-gras-ingredients-information-consumers/types-food-ingredients).

### Ginseng

NCCIH's Asian-ginseng review concerns **Panax ginseng** and particular preparations, not every botanical called ginseng or every energy-drink blend. Most clinical studies are small/short; blood-sugar findings conflict and performance findings do not support a blanket enhancement promise. Species, extract, dose, medicine interactions, and sleep effects matter. [NCCIH Asian ginseng](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng).

Snippet: “Botanical ingredient. Research on specific ginseng preparations does not establish an energy or performance benefit from this drink. It may affect sleep or interact with medicines; species, extract, and amount matter.” An unspecified “ginseng” declaration warrants limited wording, not certainty that it is Panax or a full proprietary blend reconstructed online.

### Canola/low-erucic-acid rapeseed oil

The regulation identifies low-erucic-acid rapeseed oil, also called canola oil, as an edible oil made of fatty-acid triglycerides. Fat contributes food energy; assess the drink's panel rather than inferring a heart-health therapy or harmful dose from the ingredient name. [21 CFR 184.1555](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-184/subpart-B/section-184.1555), [FDA calories and energy sources](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/calories-nutrition-facts-label).

Snippet: “Edible oil/fat ingredient. Fat contributes food energy; use the nutrition panel for this drink's total fat and calories. Presence alone does not establish a therapeutic benefit.”

### Nitrous oxide and nitrogen/aeration

FDA's substance inventory identifies nitrous oxide (N2O) as a food propellant, with its food-use regulation linked. Nitrogen (N2) is a different gas; a “nitro” description does not identify which was used. Aeration/dispensing is a product-processing function, not a nutrient benefit. [FDA nitrous oxide inventory](https://www.hfpappexternal.fda.gov/scripts/fdcc/index.cfm?id=NITROUSOXIDE&set=FoodSubstances), [FDA gases/ingredient roles](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-and-gras-ingredients-information-consumers/types-food-ingredients).

Snippet: “Gas used for aeration or dispensing, not a nutrient. The food-processing use is not the same exposure as inhaling gas.” Do not transfer recreational inhalation harms to a finished drink; if canister safety is discussed, FDA warns consumers never to inhale nitrous oxide products. [FDA inhalation warning](https://www.fda.gov/food/alerts-advisories-safety-information/fda-advises-consumers-not-inhale-nitrous-oxide-products).

### Calcium disodium EDTA

The exact FDA inventory identifies calcium disodium EDTA as a **sequestrant**. The official 2025 CFR definition specifies forming soluble complexes with metal ions for product quality/stability; this supports its metal-binding mechanism in food. The ingredient-specific regulation lists flavor retention in canned carbonated soft drinks. These sources support food-quality copy, not a bodily detoxification/medical chelation claim. [FDA EDTA identity/functions](https://www.hfpappexternal.fda.gov/scripts/fdcc/index.cfm?id=EDTACALCIUMDISODIUM&set=FoodSubstances), [2025 21 CFR 170.3(o)(26)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title21-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title21-vol3-sec170-3.pdf), [21 CFR 172.120](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-172/subpart-B/section-172.120).

Snippet: “Binds metal ions to help preserve product quality, including flavor. This is a formulation function, not evidence that the drink detoxifies the body. The ingredient statement does not disclose its concentration.” Manufacturing maxima are not measured drink exposures or personal allowances.

### Sucrose acetate isobutyrate, glycerol ester of rosin, and gellan gum

- **Sucrose acetate isobutyrate (SAIB):** Regulation supports stabilizing flavor-oil emulsions in nonalcoholic beverages. Do not classify it as a dietary sugar source merely because the name contains “sucrose.” Snippet: “Helps keep flavoring-oil emulsions stable. Its presence does not establish a nutritional benefit or a harmful dose.” [21 CFR 172.833](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-172/subpart-I/section-172.833).
- **Glycerol ester of rosin:** Regulation supports adjusting the density of citrus oils in beverages. It is not interchangeable with generic glycerin or a sweetener. Snippet: “Adjusts citrus-oil density to support the drink's formulation; concentration is not disclosed by the ingredient name.” [21 CFR 172.735](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-172/subpart-H/section-172.735).
- **Gellan gum:** A fermentation-derived polysaccharide permitted as a stabilizer/thickener. Do not infer live probiotic organisms, a declared dietary-fiber amount, or a therapeutic gut effect from its manufacture or name. Snippet: “Stabilizes or thickens the drink for consistent texture. Its presence is not a measured fiber amount or evidence of a therapeutic gut benefit.” [21 CFR 172.665](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-172/subpart-G/section-172.665).

## Transparent drinking contexts, not diet-safety verdicts

These are proposed **ClearSip interpretation rules**, not official medical categories. Show the rule and evidence basis alongside each result; multiple contexts can coexist.

- **Lower-sugar goals:** Show exact package total/added sugars when measured. Zero/low label sugar does not establish weight-loss efficacy, keto suitability, diabetes clearance, zero calories, or zero insulin response. Missing sugar data means “not assessed,” not a green badge.
- **Occasional sweetened drink:** When a verified panel shows substantial added sugar, emphasize serving size, package total, and cumulative intake. A threshold should be explicitly named an application heuristic; avoid suggesting a medically safe frequency.
- **Everyday hydration:** Restrict the positive label to plain-water records, rather than awarding it to everything whose first ingredient is water. CDC supports water for hydration and as an alternative to sugary drinks. [CDC water guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/water-healthy-drinks/index.html).
- **Caffeine awareness:** Known caffeine amount: show mg for selected package and across servings. Caffeine-containing ingredient with unknown amount: show that limitation. Do not call decaf caffeine-free or automatically exclude coffee/tea from fluid intake. [FDA caffeine guidance](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much).
- **Label-specific cautions:** PKU/aspartame, actual declared allergens, frequent acid exposure, unknown caffeine amount, and sodium/potassium context where applicable. These should remain visible even if a lower-sugar context is shown.

Footer copy: “Contexts explain label-based trade-offs, not whether a drink is safe for your personal diet. Allergies, medicines, pregnancy, medical conditions, and your total intake can change what is appropriate.”

For quantitative **label comparison**, FDA uses a 50 g added-sugars Daily Value on the general 2,000-calorie label and guides that 5% DV or less is low and 20% or more high. These translate to 2.5 g and 10 g **per labeled serving**, respectively. State this basis explicitly; a selected full package can have multiple servings. The 50 g DV is a label reference, not a recommended intake or personalized safe allowance; do not present older dietary-guideline commentary as current individualized advice. [FDA added-sugar label comparison](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/added-sugars-nutrition-facts-label).

## Evidence quality and maintenance

- Regulatory identity/labeling rules establish category and disclosure limits, not measured biological effects in a particular bottle.
- FDA intended-use safety reviews and ADIs are exposure-based, not unlimited-consumption guarantees or product-specific tests.
- NIH nutrient fact sheets support established physiological roles; supplement dosing and deficiency-treatment evidence cannot automatically be extrapolated to drinks.
- CDC/ADA summaries support frequency/exposure cautions; use association wording when supported, not invented causal dose thresholds.
- WHO explicitly marks the weight-control recommendation conditional and separate from sweetener toxicological limits.
- Animal/basic science, proprietary mixtures, unknown doses, and undisclosed flavors must retain visible uncertainty. No unsafe/healthy badge should conceal it.
- Preserve source URL, checked date, ingredient identity/aliases, evidence scope, contraindication scope, and dose-known/unknown. Re-review evolving color/additive authorizations and do not rely on a fixed generic FDA-approved badge.
